How a deliberation session works

A walkthrough of one deliberation session. The panel on the right simulates the participant's interface, and changes as you scroll.

Each session is organized around one high-level right, which sounds reasonable in the abstract but involves real trade-offs once participants decide what it should protect. This table was assigned the right below.

“People should have the right to privacy of their own data.”

Scroll to move through the steps ↓
01Opening survey · 10 min

The opening survey

Participants complete a survey privately before the discussion begins. The first question records the participant's own proposal on the topic, and it also places them in an opinion space built from the public's comments on that right, which is the position the table assignments are based on.

The participant shown here is Ray, a retired Army veteran from Texas.

The survey also records how strongly the participant feels about the topic, their agreement with a set of statements spanning the political spectrum, demographic information, and a baseline measure of polarization.

Continue to the briefing ↓
02Information session · 7 min

The information session

All participants receive the same briefing while the tables are being formed. The main point of the briefing is that a right in the Bill of Rights is a legal constraint rather than a statement of values. The Fourth Amendment does not say that privacy is important, it requires that searches be authorized by a warrant. Groups are asked to write at that level of specificity.

The briefing also tells participants that the cards their groups produce will be reviewed by the wider public.

Continue to the table assignments ↓
02·bTable assignment · concurrent

Assigning participants to tables

While participants are watching the information session, they are being sorted into tables. Each dot is one participant's survey answer, placed in the opinion space produced by the embedding. Dots that sit close together are participants who wrote similar answers.

Table composition is itself part of the experiment. Tables are assigned low, medium or high viewpoint diversity, which is visible in the shape each selection makes within the cloud. The table followed here, T5, draws its members from across the space.

Randomizing the diversity of tables allows its effects on deliberation to be measured.

Continue to the table itself ↓
03Icebreaker & guidelines · 5 min

Icebreaker and discussion guidelines

Each participant takes 30 to 45 seconds to give their name, where they are from, and what they do. They also name one discussion guideline that they consider important and explain briefly why they chose it. Asking participants to state a guideline in their own words is intended to produce more commitment to it than reading them a list of rules would.

The guidelines are adapted from the Citizens' Assembly on Brexit and from National Issues Forums materials.

Continue to the situations ↓
04·aSituations · 7 min

Sharing situations

Participants spend two minutes thinking privately about one situation, real or hypothetical, that makes this right feel important, difficult, or uncertain to them. Each person then has one uninterrupted minute to describe that situation, in clockwise order. Participants are asked to listen at this stage rather than respond to each other.

Beginning with personal experience rather than with stated opinions follows National Issues Forums and Citizens' Assembly practice, and it gives the group concrete cases that the recommendation card can refer back to.

Continue to the brainstorm ↓
04·bBrainstorm · 10 min

Brainstorming under three headings

A scribe records the discussion under three headings, which are what good the right would do, who people need protection from, and what the right could cost or where it could go wrong. Participants are asked to record specific points rather than general themes, and to record disagreements as well as areas of agreement.

The three headings correspond directly to the sections of the recommendation card, so the group is already drafting the card while it brainstorms.

Continue to the card ↓
04·cDraft the card · 11 min

Drafting the recommendation card

The group records which protections should be given, which should not be given, and which ones it remains uncertain about. Each entry carries a brief justification, which where possible refers back to a situation a participant described earlier.

Because the card requires the group to decline some protections, and provides a place for unresolved disagreement, full consensus is not required.

Recording a recommendation together with its rationale and its trade-offs follows the practice of Climate Assembly UK and of the Gdańsk citizens' assembly.

Continue to the public comments ↓
05Diffusion stress-test · 20 min

The diffusion stress test

The group is shown two comments from the public, one that bridges positions and one from the position furthest from this group. For each comment the group reads it silently, goes around once to say whether it reveals a gap, records a decision, writes a justification addressed to the commenter, and rates how useful the comment was.

Any change to the card is tagged with the comment that prompted it.

Tagging changes in this way distinguishes revisions that public input caused from revisions the group made on its own.

Continue to the exit survey ↓
06·07Reconvene · exit survey · 12 min

Reconvening and the exit survey

The group submits its card, participants reconvene, and everyone completes a short exit survey. It repeats the polarization measure from the opening survey and asks how participants feel about their group's recommendation. One available answer is that the participant does not fully agree with it but accepts it because the group arrived at it democratically.

Continue to what happens afterwards ↓
After the session

What happens to the cards

Each card becomes a portable deliberation object containing the recommendation, the lived experiences behind it, the record of what the diffusion step changed, and where each part came from.

The five cards for each right are consolidated and returned to the wider public, which is asked which protections bridge disagreement, which polarize, and which should be prioritized. The public writes the comments, small groups deliberate over them, and the public reads what the deliberation produced.
